# Lecture 18 — NLP, Linguistics, Philosophy

The last regular lecture of the course, and Christopher Manning takes the licence the title gives
him: "the lecture's titled 'NLP, Linguistics, and Philosophy,' which I took as meaning that I
could talk about anything I wanted to, and so that is what I'm going to do" (≈0:05).

What he does with it is the part of the course that is hardest to get anywhere else. Six
sections: what CS224N's major ideas actually were; what remains unsolved; an honest read on where
the best models stand; the history of the symbolic-versus-connectionist split and where language
sits in it; what *meaning* should be taken to be, which is the intellectual core of the lecture;
and a closing argument about which AI risks are worth worrying about.

There is very little architecture here and a lot of position-taking. Manning is explicit about
which claims are his own — one slide is titled "von Humboldt according to Chomsky" and the next
"von Humboldt according to me" — and this page keeps that distinction.

## What the course was about

Slides 4–8 are the retrospective (≈0:54–4:01). The arc: word vectors → neural NLP → sequence
models (RNNs, LSTMs) → the [Transformer](transformer.md) → [pre-training](09-pretraining.md)
plus [post-training](11-post-training.md) to produce foundation models → particular topics like
[benchmarking](12-benchmarking.md) and [reasoning](15-reasoning-and-agents.md).

Four ideas are singled out as the ones that did the work:

1. **Dense representations and [distributional semantics](distributional-semantics.md)** —
   representing words by their contexts, "you shall know a word by the company it keeps." Manning
   stresses this is not a neural-era idea: it "has been the idea that has driven most of the
   successful ideas of modern NLP, whether it's the earlier statistical NLP phase or the more
   modern neural NLP phase," and the same contextual principle runs from word vectors up through
   Transformers (≈2:27).
2. **Depth, and the tricks that make it trainable** — [residual connections](transformer.md)
   above all, which took the field "from a place where a lot of this seemed like black magic that
   was hard to get right" to reliably training high-performance models (≈2:27–3:14).
3. **Sequence models and computational efficiency** — the problems of recurrence, addressed by
   the Transformer's parallelism (≈3:14).
4. **[Language modelling](language-modeling.md) as a universal pre-training task** — "language
   modeling seems a simple thing — predicting words in context — but it emerges as what we think
   of as a universal pre-training task, where all kinds of both linguistic and world knowledge
   help you do this task of predicting words better," plus the empirical fact of near-linear
   gains from order-of-magnitude scaling (≈3:14–4:01).

## Open problems

Slides 9–19, one per area (≈4:47–16:22).

**Generalization versus memorization.** "There's a real question of how much these models are
good at actually learning to do things generally, rather than just being very good at
memorization." Manning's analogy: large language models are "sort of like a talking encyclopedia
… more like a huge knowledge store than necessarily something that is intelligent, in the sense
of being able to work out how to solve new problems and generalize as human beings do" (≈4:47).

Slide 10 makes the uncomfortable version of the point: on data generated by a finite automaton
and learned from limited samples, **an LSTM generalizes better than a Transformer** — "the LSTM
is basically ceiling the entire graph … whereas the Transformer needs to see a ton more data
before it actually learns the patterns well" (≈5:34–6:20). Set against the human ability to learn
a skill from a single demonstration, this is a real gap.

**Interpretability.** Neural networks "still appear as black boxes, where we have no real idea of
how they're doing what they're doing — and, as perhaps for your final projects, the main thing
you're doing is measuring the final performance number and seeing if it goes up or not" (≈7:07).
Slide 12 uses Karpathy's 2016 blog post finding an LSTM cell whose activation tracks position
along a line of text, as an early example of the mechanistic-interpretability work that has since
grown up around Transformers (≈7:53).

**Multilingual NLP.** "Whatever you see for English, it's worse for every other language"
(≈8:40). Slide 13 carries the good news — GPT-4 is better in Greek, Latvian, Arabic and Turkish
than GPT-3.5 was in English, so scaling "is, in some sense, raising all boats" — and the caveat,
which is that the bottom of that same chart holds Punjabi, Marathi and Telugu, "languages that
are each spoken by millions of people; they're not small languages" (≈9:26–10:11). The real
problem is the long tail: thousands of languages with hundreds to hundreds of thousands of
speakers, mostly oral, with too little text for any of this to apply, and many facing extinction
in the coming decades (≈10:58–11:44).

**Benchmark integrity.** The worry is that "all of the benchmarks are being sullied and are not
to be trusted." Slide 16 quotes Horace He on GPT-4 and Codeforces: it solved 10 out of 10 easy
*pre-2021* problems and **zero out of 10 recent ones**, which "strongly points to contamination."
So much text is on the web that models are "memorizing at least a good share of the questions,"
and the open question is what thoroughly hidden test sets or dynamic evaluation mechanisms could
restore integrity (≈12:31–13:16). See [benchmark contamination](benchmark-contamination.md).

**Domains.** Biomedical and clinical NLP have their own vocabulary and usage, and real potential
for harm when understanding is incomplete. Manning's own involvement is in legal NLP, with
Stanford's RegLab: the motivating problem is access — "most people can't get access to the kind
of legal help that they need … because of the cost of it and the lack of trained lawyers," worse
in India than in the US — and the blocker is reliability. A then-just-completed RegLab study found
a hallucination rate of roughly **one answer in six** containing made-up material, "which isn't a
very good accuracy rate if you're someone who's wanting to rely on these systems for legal
advice" (≈14:03–15:35).

**Social aspects.** Systems "remain very biased against various cultures and religions. They have
certain social norms, you could say, that they pick up from somewhere, but those social norms are
very biased against certain groups" (≈15:35).

## Where we actually are with LLMs

Manning's own reaction first (slide 20, ≈16:22–19:31). He asked ChatGPT-4o that morning for a
sonnet explaining the Transformer architecture in which every line begins with the letter T, and
got one — imperfect rhymes ("net"/"set") but real ones ("behold"/"unfold", "grasp"/"clasp"),
roughly iambic pentameter, every line starting with T. "It still, frankly, blows my mind, and I
don't actually feel I can really explain, even to myself, in a way that's convincing, how this
large Transformer is able to take all its pre-training text, and reading that instruction, and,
as a next-token prediction machine, it successfully produces something that is a sonnet."

Then the evidence, in both directions:

- **Consultants using GPT-4 do better work** (slide 21, ≈20:17–22:40). Dell'Acqua, Mollick and
  colleagues gave Boston Consulting Group consultants a controlled task. Three groups: two using
  GPT-4 (one with extra training on how to use it, which made little difference) and one without.
  The GPT-4 groups completed **12% more tasks**, worked **25% faster**, and produced results
  judged **40% higher quality**. Manning calls it "a pretty stunning success, of how GPT-4, or
  similar LLMs, are good enough to actually help people get real work done." The secondary
  finding matters as much: LLMs are "a big leveler," helping weaker performers far more than
  strong ones — the same pattern seen with coding assistants. (The slide's density plot also
  shows the AI-using group is *bimodal*, with a small cluster of much worse outcomes.)
- **GPT-4 cannot write like a professional** (slide 22, ≈22:40). Asked whether it matches *New
  Yorker* fiction writers: "not even close" — measured as three to ten times worse at creative
  writing. "So there's still hope for human beings — hang on there."
- **The market view is not uniformly bullish** (slide 23, ≈23:25–25:00). The *Financial Times*
  Lex column, "Generative AI: hypely intelligent," argues expectations run "way ahead of the
  limitations," notes IDC's forecast of enterprise spending reaching **$143 billion by 2027 from
  $16 billion**, and lands on the line Manning highlights: "models predict, they do not
  comprehend."

## Symbolic AI, cybernetics, and where language sits

This is the historical section (slides 24–34), and its payoff is a precise position on a question
students usually get sloganeering about.

**Two traditions.** Symbolic methods "dominated AI from the '60s until about 2010," but the
original alternative, going back to the '50s, was **cybernetics** — and "in a very real sense,
neural networks is a continuation of the cybernetics tradition, rather than the AI tradition"
(≈25:46). John McCarthy, a mathematician and logician who founded Stanford's AI Lab, coined the
term *artificial intelligence* precisely "to disassociate what he was doing from the cybernetics
approach," pursued by Norbert Wiener at MIT (≈28:51). Cybernetics came from control and
communication, closer to electrical engineering; its Greek root *kybernetes* also gives us
*Kubernetes* and, through Latin, *government* — "of course, it's a control system as well"
(≈31:11).

Stanford's **Symbolic Systems** program is an artifact of that history. Jon Barwise (1942–2000)
"had a very strong belief that you needed to be dealing with meaning in the world, and the
connection between people's thinking and the world," and refused to let the program be called
cognitive science (≈26:32). Where cognitive science studies mind and intelligence as naturally
occurring phenomena, Symbolic Systems "gives equal focus to human-constructed systems that use
symbols" — languages, logics, programming languages — and to the systems that process them
(slide 24).

**The physical symbol system hypothesis.** Newell and Simon's claim: a physical symbol system
"has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action." Manning flags how strong
that is — the *necessary* clause says you cannot have general intelligence **without** a symbol
system (≈30:25).

**Early hype is not new.** Slide 27 reproduces the *New York Times* of 8 July 1958 on
Rosenblatt's Perceptron: "the Navy revealed the embryo of an electronic computer today that it
expects will be able to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself, and be conscious of its
existence." What was actually demonstrated: the machine learned to tell right-pointing from
left-pointing arrows after fifty exposures (≈32:45–33:31).

**Manning's resolution** (slide 28, ≈33:31–35:48) separates two claims that are usually run
together:

> Language **is** a symbolic system. In writing this is obvious, but it holds for speech too —
> the phones of a language are discrete categories, recognized categorically by language users,
> and categorical perception in cognitive psychology was worked out on exactly this material.
>
> But **the processor need not be**. "The fact that humans use a symbol system for communication
> doesn't mean that the process that processes the symbols — the human brain — has to be a
> physical symbol system, and so, similarly, we don't have to design our NLP computer processors
> as physical symbol systems either."

That is a direct rejection of the *necessary* half of Newell and Simon. And it comes with a
hypothesis about why language is symbolic at all: **signalling reliability**. "We could have just
hummed at different frequencies" — discrete, well-separated targets are what let a degraded
signal be recovered (≈35:48).

**So what is linguistics for?** Not for its fine-grained theories: "I don't think one necessarily
wants to believe all the fine details of different linguistic theories," but "most of our broad
understanding of linguistics is right" (slides 29–31, ≈36:34–38:52). Its role is to supply "the
questions, concepts, and distinctions" — and increasingly to supply the *evaluation* vocabulary
for neural systems: sentence structure, discourse structure, natural language inference, bridging
anaphora, metaphor, presupposition. Slide 30 lists the concepts now central to deep-learning
research: **compositionality, systematic generalization, stable meanings for symbols,
manipulating reference**. His framing: early neural work on vision and sound "gets you to
insect-level intelligence," and going beyond that is where linguistic properties start to matter
(≈37:20–38:05).

**Language as a thinking tool.** Two arguments, and Manning marks the second as his own reading:

- **Wilhelm von Humboldt (1767–1835)**, as Chomsky cites him, for the claim that language makes
  "infinite use of finite means" — finite words and structures recursively yielding infinitely
  many sentences, in support of a structured symbolic view (slide 32, ≈41:13).
- **Von Humboldt "according to me"** (slide 33): language is not merely an outward manifestation
  of thought for communication — "language is no product (*Ergon*), but an activity (*Energeia*)"
  — and effectively distinguishes what we would now call System 1 from System 2 cognition,
  calling the former "acts of the spirit." The claim Manning has sympathy with is that System 2
  thought *requires* "the fruitful extension of the mind through the symbols of language," making
  language "the necessary foundation for the progress of the human mind." His own gloss: "we can
  think without language — we can feel afraid, we can think visually … but for the sort of more
  abstract, larger-scale thinking that humans engage in, language gives a scaffolding inside the
  mind that makes that possible" (≈41:59–43:34).
- **Dennett's four grades** (slide 34, ≈44:20–47:32), from *From Bacteria to Bach and Back*, as a
  ladder of increasing sample efficiency and competence: **Darwinian** (fixed competence, improved
  only by selection — bacteria), **Skinnerian** (learns from reinforcement — a lizard or dog),
  **Popperian** (builds environment models, so can plan — chimpanzees, and, Manning notes with
  amusement at the literature's geography, Southern Hemisphere crows that learn multi-stage plans
  to poke meat out of a hole), and **Gregorian** (builds *thinking tools*). Mathematics is a
  thinking tool; so, arguably, is democracy; "but out of the space of thinking tools, human
  language is the preeminent thinking tool that we have," and humans are the only biological
  Gregorian intelligence.

## What is meaning?

The intellectual centre of the lecture (slides 35–48), and the place where CS224N's own
foundations get examined rather than assumed.

**The two theories** (slide 35, ≈48:19):

- **Model-theoretic (denotational) semantics** — the meaning of a word is its denotation in (a
  model of) the world. The meaning of *computer* is the set of computers.
- **[Distributional semantics](distributional-semantics.md)** — understanding a word's meaning is
  understanding the contexts it occurs in. This is what word vectors and all their descendants
  implement.

The first "was the notion that was used in most of the history of AI for doing symbolic AI"
(≈48:19). See [formal semantics](formal-semantics.md) for the full development.

**The traditional pipeline.** Slide 36 shows the target: *The red apple is on the table* becomes

$$on(\iota(\lambda x(apple(x) \wedge red(x))),\, \iota(\lambda y.\, table(y)))$$

and asks the question that matters — "but how do we get the latter from the former? Other than by
setting undergrads to work…" In an intro logic class "you have some English sentences which you
translate into formal logic, and then, after that, you forget about human languages, and you just
start proving stuff about formal logical systems" (≈49:53).

That was Tarski's position, and it was the dominant one: from about 1940 to 1980 Tarski was the
preeminent logician in the US, and he held that meaning could not be discussed in terms of human
languages "because human languages were, quote, 'impossibly incoherent'" (≈50:40). **Richard
Montague**, his student, rebelled: "I reject the contention that an important theoretical
difference exists between formal and natural languages" (slide 37). Montague's work became the
foundation of linguistic semantics, and the resulting picture "was essentially used as the model
of doing natural-language understanding for most of the history of NLP, roughly 1960 to 2015,
2017" (≈51:27).

Slides 40–41 work the derivation through: parse the sentence, look each word's meaning up in a
lexicon, then compose upward through the tree with rule-to-rule composition, so *the* is
$\lambda P.\iota(P)$, *red* is $\lambda P.\lambda x(P(x) \wedge red(x))$, and the pieces combine
to the logical form above. Slide 42 shows the industrial version — *How many red cars in Palo
Alto does Kathy like?* parsed to a lambda expression and compiled to SQL to run against a
database — with the note that the "zenith" of the approach was **semantic parsing** (Zettlemoyer
& Collins 2005; Artzi & Zettlemoyer 2013; Liang, Jordan & Klein 2013), where the parser, word
meanings and composition rules were *learned* rather than hand-written. Manning's verdict: "these
systems could actually work, and were used in limited domains, but they're always extremely
brittle" (≈55:19).

**Two properties worth stealing** (slide 38, ≈37:20 and ≈53:01):

- **[Compositionality](compositionality.md)**, "Frege's principle" — "though very unclear that he
  either said or believed in it" — that the meaning of a whole is a function only of the meanings
  of its syntactic parts and the way they are combined. Exploiting it "can give an exponential
  gain in representational power."
- **Systematic generalization** (Fodor & Pylyshyn 1988) — if you can interpret a noun phrase in
  subject position you should also be able to interpret it in object position. Children of 2
  years 11 months can do this (Brooks & Tomasello 1999).

**Do humans do it this way?** Slide 43 says there is evidence that "to get the meaning of a
sentence, humans also perform computations that are hierarchical, following mostly projective
bottom-up trees," citing Crain and Nakayama (1987), Pallier et al. (2011), Ding et al. (2016) and
Hale et al. (2018). Manning is careful: "there's a lot of controversy as to exactly how human
understanding of sentences works," but this "is obviously not what we're getting with present-day
Transformers" (≈55:19–56:07).

Which sets up slide 44, a whole page containing one handwritten question: **do neural models
provide suitable meaning (composition) functions?** His answer is genuinely mixed — "in many
ways, they seem to — they do an amazing job at understanding whatever sentences you put into them
— but there are still some genuine concerns as to whether they're taking shortcuts … and don't
actually have the same kind of compositional understanding, with systematic generalization, that
human beings do" (≈56:07).

**The use theory of meaning.** The other tradition, usually attributed in NLP to J. R. Firth's
"you shall know a word by the company it keeps," has a philosophical parent in the later
Wittgenstein (slide 45, ≈56:54–58:28). Two quotations from *Philosophical Investigations*, and
Manning's gloss on each. On the logicians' project:

> When I talk about language (words, sentences, etc.) I must speak the language of every day. Is
> this language somehow too coarse and material for what we want to say? Then how is another one
> to be constructed?—And how strange that we should be able to do anything at all with the one we
> have!

— that is, "isn't that a weird concept, that one symbol system is no good, but this other symbol
system somehow fixes things?" And on denotation:

> You say: the point isn't the word, but its meaning, and you think of the meaning as a thing of
> the same kind as the word, though also different from the word. Here the word, there the
> meaning. The money, and the cow that you can buy with it. (But contrast: money, and its use.)

The meaning of money is what money does, not the physical notes it points at.

**Manning's own position** (slide 46, ≈59:14–1:00:01). Some people reject distributional meaning
as meaning at all — "most prominently, in recent NLP work, that's the position of Bender and
Koller," who "take as axiomatic that the only thing that counts as having a meaning is that
you've got form over here and meaning over there." He thinks that is too narrow, and states two
claims:

1. **Meaning arises from connecting words to other things.** The real world is privileged but not
   the only grounding — virtual worlds count, and so do connections to other language.
2. **Meaning is gradient**, not zero-one. You can understand a word more or less well.

**The shehnai argument** (slides 47–48, ≈1:00:01–1:03:12), from Manning's 2022 *Dædalus* paper
*Human Language Understanding and Reasoning*, is the concrete case for both claims. What is the
meaning of *shehnai*?

- Having seen or held one gives the classic grounded meaning.
- A picture gives you some of it; having heard one played, more.
- Being told it is "a traditional Indian instrument, a bit like an oboe" already gives you
  something real — a connection to India, to reed wind instruments, to music.
- Adding that it has holes like a recorder but multiple reeds and a flared end gives you more.
- And a single example of the word in use gives you something the other routes do not. From
  Anuradha Roy's *An Atlas of Impossible Longing*: "From a week before, shehnai players sat in
  bamboo machans at the entrance to the house, playing their pipes. Bikash Babu disliked the
  shehnai's wail, but was determined to fulfil every conventional expectation the groom's family
  might have."

The punchline is not that text is as good as grounding — it is that the orderings are
incomparable. "In some ways you understand less of the meaning of the word than if you'd seen
one, but, actually, in other ways, you understand more … because, from that one textual example,
you know some things — you have heard a characterization of the sound as wailing, and that it's
connected with weddings, which you don't get from just having held or looked at one, or even
having had someone stand in front of you and play it. And that's an important part of the meaning
of 'shehnai' to people."

## Our AI future

The closing section (slides 49–64), and Manning's ordering of the risks is the argument.

**Will we lose our jobs?** Slide 50 reproduces the *New York Times* of **1928**: "March of the
Machine Makes Idle Hands — Prevalence of Unemployment with Greatly Increased Industrial Output
Points to the Influence of Labor-Saving Devices as an Underlying Cause." Slide 51 is *Time*, 1961:
"In the past, new industries hired far more people than those they put out of business. But this
is not true of many of today's new industries." Manning's read: "this is a longstanding fear
which, at least so far, has not been realized," and the promised three-day week never arrived
(≈1:04:03–1:05:37).

**Will the money all go to five or ten technology giants?** "I actually think this is a more
serious worry — this seems to be the direction that we're headed in at the moment." The historical
analogy is transportation: domination of new railway networks concentrated economic power in a few
hands in the early twentieth century, and post-Depression political action broke it. "That's a
political problem to solve, rather than actually being a technological problem to solve" — with
the caveat that "there's not much sign of political leadership right at the moment"
(≈1:05:37–1:07:11).

**Should we fear the singularity?** Slide 54 collects the x-risk headlines — "Pausing AI
Developments Isn't Enough, We Need to Shut It All Down," "AI 'Godfather' Geoffrey Hinton Warns of
Dangers as He Quits Google" — and Manning says plainly: "I don't personally give these concerns
too much credence" (≈1:08:00). Slide 55 gives the critics:

- **François Chollet**: "there does not exist any AI model or technique that could represent an
  extinction risk for humanity, not even if you extrapolate capabilities far into the future via
  scaling laws. Most arguments boil down to: this is a new type of technology, it could happen."
- **Joelle Pineau** calls the discourse "unhinged," and Manning spells out the logical flaw he
  thinks matters: if extinction is treated as *infinitely* bad, then any nonzero probability times
  infinity dominates every other consideration — "that isn't actually a sensible way to have
  rational discussion about the outcomes" (≈1:09:34).
- **Timnit Gebru** and others argue the focus on existential risk distracts — "and, if you're more
  cynical, a lot of the *purpose* of this focus" is to distract — from immediate harms: bias,
  worker exploitation, copyright violation, disinformation, concentration of power, and regulatory
  capture (≈1:10:19).

**The harms he does weight** (slides 56–58): disinformation, deception, hallucination, homogeneity
of decision-making, copyright and creative displacement, carbon emissions, erosion of rich human
practices. For NLP specifically, offensive content, untruthful content, and disinformation — with
a sharpened version of the last: "if models can reason well about text, can they also be
persuasive in communicating incorrect information … perhaps there are new possibilities for doing
very personalized misinformation propagation that more easily persuades human beings than
traditional methods of political advertising." Multiple studies now suggest humans are influenced
by AI-generated disinformation, and "perhaps the worst of it isn't going to be text-based — it's
likely that visual fakes are going to be even more compelling in a political context"
(≈1:11:05–1:12:36).

**The reframing** is the sentence to take away (≈1:12:36):

> what we should be doing is worrying not about existential risks, but worrying about what people
> and organizations with power will use AI to do.

With social media as the precedent: it "was meant to lead to new freedoms for people across the
globe … that isn't what's happened — new technologies get captured by powerful people and
organizations who master the new technological options, and AI and machine learning is being
increasingly used for surveillance and control" (≈1:13:24).

**The closing quotation** is Carl Sagan, from *The Demon-Haunted World* (slide 64,
≈1:14:58):

> I have a foreboding of a world in my children's or grandchildren's time — when awesome
> technological powers are in the hands of a very few, and no one representing the public
> interest can even grasp the issues; when the people have lost the ability to set their own
> agendas or knowledgeably question those in authority; when, clutching our crystals and
> nervously consulting our horoscopes, our critical faculties in decline, unable to distinguish
> between what feels good and what's true, we slide, almost without noticing, back into
> superstition and darkness.

Manning's last words to the class: this "is actually much more the risk that humanity is facing,
and why education, which we try to provide at Stanford and other places, is an important thing
that should be valued, and all the other things that go along with this, of having things like
open source, that supports the broad dissemination of learning" (≈1:15:44).
